Most AI projects report good numbers early. More people log in, more tasks run through the tool, and someone estimates the hours saved. Then the money is approved for the next phase, and a year later it is hard to say what changed.

The early numbers were not wrong. They were measuring use, and use is easy to grow. What a leader needs to know is whether the business is making better decisions, and whether it could keep doing so on its own.

## Why adoption numbers mislead

Usage rises for many reasons: a launch email, a mandate from the top, a novelty that wears off. Time saved is usually an estimate made by the people with a stake in the project. Neither tells you whether the work got better or what the saved time was spent on.

Both can look healthy while every important decision is made exactly as before.

## Four checks

Before you spend more, ask four questions about the project.

1. **Does it run on real work?** Demonstrations use tidy examples. Real work is messy, late and full of exceptions. A project that has only seen tidy examples has not been tested.
2. **Can the people using it correct it?** If a wrong answer needs the builder to fix it, the project depends on the builder. Good systems let users correct them in plain words and keep the correction.
3. **Would it survive the builder leaving?** Imagine the team that built it leaves next week. If the system would quietly stop being used, you have bought a service.
4. **Will the owner of the result vouch for it?** Ask the person accountable for the outcome whether they would recommend it to a peer, out loud. Hesitation is data.

A project that passes all four is worth scaling. One that fails two or more needs fixing before it gets more money.

## Make the first version small enough to prove

The easiest way to pass these checks is to start small. Pick one decision or capability, build a working first version, use it on real work and keep what works. A small system that a leader will vouch for teaches you more than a large one nobody owns.
