For years a data company could sell the analysis. A buyer paid for the dashboard, the report or the benchmark, because turning raw data into an answer took skilled people and time.

That part is getting cheap. A buyer's own AI can now read a dataset and produce a passable report in minutes. Some of what a data company used to charge for, the buyer can now make for itself.

## What still holds its value

Three things are hard for a buyer's AI to copy.

**Rights and a known source.** Data that is exclusive, licensed cleanly and kept current is worth more as AI spreads, because every model needs inputs it can trust. A buyer cannot prompt their way to data they have no right to.

**A place in the decision.** A data product that sits inside how a customer decides, in their planning, their pricing or their buying, is hard to remove. One that produces a report someone reads once a month is easy to replace.

**Judgement about what it means.** Knowing which number matters for this customer, this quarter, is still scarce. Data companies that can say "here is what this means for you" keep a seat in the room.

## Change the price and the pitch

If the analysis is cheap, pricing by report or by seat starts to look strange to buyers. The price has to follow something the buyer values: access to rights they cannot get elsewhere, or the decision the data improves.

The pitch changes too. Sellers who used to show what the data says now have to show what decision it changed and what happened next. That often means a different buyer inside the customer, and a different team to sell to them.

## Where to start

Pick one product line and ask four questions about it. What do we make that a buyer's AI cannot? What should it cost now? How do we explain why it matters? Who in our team should sell it, and with what help from AI? The answers rarely leave all four levers where they were.
